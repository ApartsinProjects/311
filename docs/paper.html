A Multi-City Free-Text Benchmark for 311 Service-Request Classification and Cross-Jurisdiction Transfer

A. Partsin
Working draft, August 2026 · Code and data: github.com/ApartsinProjects/311

Abstract

Municipal 311 systems record millions of citizen service requests as free text, yet public research uses single-city datasets with private splits and incomparable metrics, and no canonical multi-city free-text 311 benchmark exists. We assemble the first public multi-city free-text 311 corpus, drawing citizen narratives from seven US cities, and map each city's native service taxonomy (545 distinct categories) into a shared 14-class civic ontology so that models can be evaluated across jurisdictions. On this benchmark we measure a large and consistent cross-jurisdiction generalization gap: a strong TF-IDF classifier scores macro-F1 0.80 within a city but 0.38 when trained on other cities and tested on a held-out one. A large language model prompted zero-shot with the taxonomy transfers better than the frozen-label classical model (macro-F1 0.49) while remaining well below in-domain performance. A per-example error analysis shows that much of the measured gap is a benchmark artifact rather than model failure: roughly half of the errors have the predicted topic present in the text, arising from service-category versus content mismatch, source mislabeling, and uninformative shorthand. We conclude that a naive multi-city 311 benchmark measures taxonomy non-comparability and label noise as much as generalization, and we separate these components as the benchmark's core contribution.

1. Introduction

311 is the non-emergency municipal service channel used across the United States and Canada. Residents report potholes, missed garbage collection, graffiti, broken streetlights, illegal dumping, encampments, and hundreds of other conditions, often as free-text descriptions. These narratives are a large, renewable source of civic text with direct operational value: routing a request to the right department, prioritizing it, and detecting duplicates all begin with understanding what the text says.

Two obstacles have kept 311 free text underused in NLP research. First, most large open-data portals publish only a structured category dropdown and omit the citizen narrative, so the text that would make the problem an NLP problem is frequently unavailable. Second, the work that does use 311 text trains and evaluates on a single city with a private split, so results are not comparable across studies and say nothing about whether a model transfers to a new city with a different taxonomy and reporting style.

This paper builds the missing benchmark and uses it to ask a question that single-city work cannot: how well does a 311 text classifier generalize across jurisdictions? We assemble free-text requests from seven cities, harmonize their taxonomies into a shared label space, and evaluate under a leave-one-city-out protocol. We report three findings. The cross-jurisdiction gap is large and consistent. An LLM prompted with the taxonomy transfers better than a classical model whose label space is fixed to the training cities. And a substantial share of the measured gap is attributable to label artifacts rather than model error, which reframes what a multi-city 311 benchmark actually measures.

2. Related work

Applied 311 classification is dominated by complaint-type and department routing, where classical TF-IDF pipelines and, more recently, transformer encoders reach accuracies in the seventies to eighties on a single city's descriptions. A separate mature line studies 311 reporting equity using structured propensity data rather than the text. Adjacent civic-text corpora such as SeeClickFix, FixMyStreet, and consumer-complaint collections have supported urgency scoring and multimodal routing. Across this literature, evaluation is single-city and metrics are computed on private splits; no shared multi-city free-text 311 benchmark, leaderboard, or released model exists. We fill that gap and add the cross-jurisdiction transfer question.

3. The benchmark

3.1 The free-text availability problem

The first design constraint is that genuine citizen narrative is scarce across cities. We verified by direct probing that the largest portals (New York, Chicago, Los Angeles, Dallas, Washington) expose only structured category fields; columns that appear textual, such as descriptor or resolution_description, are templated dropdown values, not narratives. Free text lives instead in the Open311 API description field and in the app-based platforms that some mid-size cities republish to their open portals. We assembled the corpus from the sources where a populated citizen narrative was confirmed.

3.3 Harmonized taxonomy

The seven cities use 545 distinct native categories, ranging from Chicago-style broad groups to San Francisco's 176 service names. We map every native category into a shared 14-class civic ontology: Waste and Sanitation, Streets and Sidewalks, Street Lighting, Traffic Signals and Signs, Trees and Vegetation, Graffiti and Postings, Parking and Vehicles, Property and Housing Code, Water Sewer and Drainage, Homelessness, Animals and Pests, Noise, Transit, and Parks and Recreation. Administrative and routing-only categories, such as San Francisco's per-department "General Request" tags, form a fifteenth bucket that we exclude from the classification task. The mapping is an ordered, first-match rule set applied to the native category string, which keeps it transparent and auditable; it covers 80 to 95 percent of records per city as content classes.

4. Experimental setup

Task. Given the citizen free text, predict one of the 14 harmonized content classes.

Protocols. In-city uses a stratified 80/20 split within a city and bounds performance without domain shift. Leave-one-city-out (LOCO) trains on all other cities and tests on the held-out city, which is the cross-jurisdiction transfer number.

Metric. Macro-F1 over the classes present in the test set, computed identically for every model so the arms are directly comparable.

Models. A TF-IDF classifier combining word unigrams and bigrams with character 3-to-5-grams, fed to a logistic-regression head. A fine-tuned DistilBERT encoder. And a large language model (gpt-4o-mini) prompted zero-shot with the 14-class taxonomy and short glosses, which sees no city-specific training data and is therefore an inherently cross-jurisdiction condition.

The results are consistent across models. Crossing city boundaries roughly halves macro-F1 for both trained models, from 0.80 to 0.38 for TF-IDF and from 0.77 to 0.41 for DistilBERT, and the drop holds for every held-out city. The mechanism is visible in the per-city breakdown: the cross-city classifier collapses toward the majority class, so its accuracy approaches the majority-class rate while its macro-F1 falls. The LLM, given only the taxonomy and no training data, reaches macro-F1 0.49 across cities, above both frozen-label trained models and below in-domain performance. Giving a model the target label space in context therefore helps where a fixed label space does not, but does not close the gap.

6. What the gap measures

Because a halving of performance is a strong claim, we read the individual failures. On a balanced sample the LLM makes errors on about 22 percent of requests, and roughly half of those errors have the predicted topic literally present in the text. The failures fall into a small number of structural categories.

  • Service-category versus content mismatch, the dominant cause. A request labeled MISSED WOODY WASTE SERVICE (mapped to Waste and Sanitation) reads "tree limbs that were not picked up", which a text model naturally assigns to Trees and Vegetation. Both readings are defensible; the city label names a collection service while the text names the material.
  • Source mislabeling. The city's own label is sometimes wrong for the text, for example the text "graffiti" filed under Sidewalk Repair, or a pipe-and-hole report filed under Sidewalk Snow Removal.
  • Uninformative shorthand. Call-center entries such as B/U, o.w., and no sticker carry no signal in the text; the label encodes context the text does not.
  • Genuinely multi-topic requests that a single label cannot represent, such as a junk car, a fallen tent, and a discarded door in one report.

The measured cross-jurisdiction gap therefore conflates three distinct effects: genuine domain shift between cities, non-comparability of the harmonized taxonomy at its boundaries, and irreducible noise in the city-assigned labels. Separating them is the benchmark's central methodological contribution, and it points to concrete refinements: a defensibility-adjusted metric that credits acceptable alternative labels, a taxonomy revision that resolves the service-versus-content boundary by intent, a text-informativeness filter that removes shorthand entries, and rule- or example-augmented prompting that specifies the boundaries the model is expected to honor.

7. Limitations and scope

The corpus covers seven cities, weighted toward small and mid-size municipalities where free text is openly available; the two largest contributors, Baton Rouge and Bloomington, dominate the raw volume, and we cap per-city counts to balance training. San Francisco contributes few content rows because its open narrative volume is rate-limited and its taxonomy is administrative, so its per-city numbers are the least stable. The harmonized taxonomy is one defensible mapping among several, and its boundaries are exactly where the error analysis locates disagreement. Labels are the cities' own routing categories and carry the noise documented in Section 6.

8. Conclusion

We built the first public multi-city free-text 311 benchmark and used it to measure cross-jurisdiction transfer. A strong classical model loses roughly forty macro-F1 points when it moves to an unseen city, an LLM prompted with the taxonomy transfers better than a frozen-label model, and a careful reading of the failures shows that much of the gap reflects taxonomy non-comparability and label noise rather than model error. The benchmark, its harmonization, and the evaluation code are public so that cross-jurisdiction 311 classification can be studied on comparable ground.

Reproducibility

All code, the harmonized data, the taxonomy mapping, and the logged results are at github.com/ApartsinProjects/311. The pipeline runs as collect_311.py → harmonize.py → the three evaluation arms, with every run recorded to results/summary.csv.
